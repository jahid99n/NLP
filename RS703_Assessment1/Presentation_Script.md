# RS703 Assessment 1: Presentation Script

**Target time:** about 4 min 45 s (around 630 words at a relaxed pace)
**Say it like:** "Petrič" = *PET-rich*

---

## Slide 1: Title (about 15 s)

Hi everyone. Today I'm looking at a 2025 study by Petrič and Just. It asks a simple question: why do some employees report phishing emails, and others don't?

My short answer is: it's a well-built study, but it can't prove as much as it seems to.

---

## Slide 2: The study at a glance (about 35 s)

Most research on phishing asks who *clicks* on the bad link. This study asks a different question: who *reports* it?

That matters, because every report is an early warning. It lets the security team stop an attack before it costs the company money.

The authors surveyed just over a thousand employees in Germany, the UK and the US. They tested whether four parts of a company's security culture make people more likely to report.

---

## Slide 3: The model (about 40 s)

Here's their model. On the left are the four parts of security culture: knowing the policies, good communication, security knowledge, and team habits that support security.

The idea is that these shape how people *feel* about security, and whether they feel it's *their job*. And that leads to reporting.

Mostly, that's what they found. But there's a twist. Good communication made people report more, but it also made them feel *less* personally responsible. Maybe they think, "IT has this covered."

The red badges show four weak spots. Let's go through them.

---

## Slide 4: Methodology scorecard (about 50 s)

First, the design. It's a one-time survey. That's fine for testing a theory. But the model says A leads to B, and B leads to C. You can't show that order from one snapshot in time.

Second, the sample. People came from an online survey panel. They chose to sign up, so they may not be typical workers.

Third, the data. Everything came from one questionnaire, even whether people report. People like to give answers that make them look good, so the numbers may be a bit too rosy.

Fourth, the statistics. This is the strongest part. They checked that the questions meant the same thing in all three countries. You need that before you can fairly compare countries.

---

## Slide 5: Sampling (about 30 s)

Let's look closer at the sample. The US group has over 500 people. The UK group has just over 200. So the UK results are less certain.

Also, each person seems to come from a different company. But culture is something a whole team shares. Asking one person per company tells us what *that person* thinks, not what the culture really is.

---

## Slide 6: Strengths vs limitations (about 45 s)

So, what's good and what's not?

On the plus side, the model is clear and based on theory. The sample is big, it covers three countries, and the authors are honest about their weak spots.

On the downside, we can't tell cause from effect. Maybe people who report a lot start to care more, not the other way round. Saying you report isn't the same as actually reporting. We only have one point in time. With over a thousand people, even tiny effects look "significant". And it only covers big Western companies, so we can't say much about the rest of the world.

---

## Slide 7: Improvements (about 45 s)

So how could we fix this? I have four ideas.

One: measure what people actually *do*. Many companies send fake phishing emails to test their staff. We could link the survey to who really reported them.

Two: survey people at three different times, so causes come before effects.

Three: ask several people in each company, so we measure culture as something shared.

Four: measure cultural values directly, and include smaller and non-Western companies.

These fixes cost more. Fake phishing tests also raise ethical questions, because staff are being tricked. But they would give us much stronger evidence.

---

## Slide 8: Conclusion (about 25 s)

To wrap up: this is a useful study. It treats employees as defenders, not just the weak link.

But it shows links between what people *think*, not what actually drives what they *do*.

For business research, the lesson is simple. Surveys are great for spotting patterns, but to prove cause, you need stronger designs.

Thank you. I'm happy to take any questions.

---

## Delivery tips

- Slow down on the key lines: your verdict (slide 1), the twist (slide 3), and the lesson (slide 8).
- Point at the slide as you talk: the badges on slide 3, the UK bar on slide 5.
- Don't read word for word. Learn the first line of each slide, then speak freely.
- Time two full practice runs. If you're over 5 minutes, shorten slide 6 first.

## Likely questions, with short answers

**"Why did you pick this article?"**
It links to my field, phishing detection, and it's a business problem. It also has a rich survey method to critique.

**"What's the single biggest weakness?"**
Reporting is self-reported. We don't know if people actually report. Linking the survey to real report logs would fix that.

**"Isn't a one-time survey good enough?"**
It's fine for finding links. But this model claims one thing leads to another, and you need data from more than one time point to show that.
